## 1. Aim and scope

This experiment evaluates the computational performance of the
diary-data aggregation pipeline used in our proposed study of
short-form video use and student procrastination.

We compare the existing implementation with an alternative grouped
aggregation. Both produce participant-level variables X, S, B, P, L
and A. This experiment uses synthetic data and does not test the
behavioural hypotheses about students.

## 2. Compared methods

The baseline performs separate grouped aggregations and merges
their results into one participant-level table.

The proposed implementation calculates the same aggregate variables
within one groupby aggregation call.

Before timing, output equivalence was checked for every workload
using pandas.testing.assert_frame_equal. All checks passed.

## 3. Experimental setup

The experiment ran on an Apple M1 computer with 8 GiB RAM.
Software versions and configuration are recorded in the experiment
metadata.

Four workloads contained 10, 100, 1,000 and 10,000 synthetic rows.
Larger workloads were generated by repeating the sample with unique
participant identifiers.

Each method completed 10 warm-up and 100 measured runs per workload.
Method order was randomized within paired repetitions. The random
seed was fixed at 42.

Only aggregation-function execution was timed using
time.perf_counter_ns(). CSV reading, workload generation, output
verification, console output and file writing were outside timing.

## 4. Data processing and statistical methods

The comparison log contains 880 records: 80 warm-up records and
800 measured records. Warm-up records were excluded from analysis.

For each method and workload, we calculated mean, sample standard
deviation, median, p25, p75, p95 and p99.

Percentile-bootstrap 95% confidence intervals were calculated for
the median, p95 and p99 using 5,000 resamples and seed 42.

Execution-time statistics describe successful measured runs.
Failures remain included in the error-rate denominator.
All 800 measured runs succeeded, giving an observed error rate of 0%.

## 5. Results

Values are median (p25–p75), in milliseconds.

| Input size (rows) | Baseline, ms | Proposed, ms | Speedup |
|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| 10 | 3.537 (3.517–3.571) | 1.910 (1.903–1.920) | 1.85× |
| 100 | 3.635 (3.622–3.658) | 1.929 (1.921–1.938) | 1.88× |
| 1,000 | 5.016 (4.991–5.171) | 2.193 (2.176–2.291) | 2.29× |
| 10,000 | 20.647 (20.481–21.025) | 4.852 (4.797–4.975) | 4.26× |

Speedup is the baseline median divided by the proposed median,
calculated before rounding.

The proposed implementation had lower median execution time at
every tested workload. At 10,000 rows, median execution time decreased
from approximately 20.647 ms to 4.852 ms.

Full statistics and confidence intervals are available in
[data/processed/metrics_summary.csv](../data/processed/metrics_summary.csv).

## 9. Limitations

Results come from one session on one computer and repeated synthetic
sample data. Increasing workload size increased both the number of
rows and the number of participant groups.

Bootstrap intervals assume independent runs and describe
within-session uncertainty. Background OS activity and dependence
between successive timings may affect estimates. The intervals do
not capture variation between machines or separate sessions.

With 100 measurements per group, p99 estimates are approximate.
No memory-usage comparison was conducted.

Output equivalence was established on the tested workloads; this
does not prove correctness for every possible input.

## 10. Conclusion

The proposed aggregation produced matching outputs and lower median
execution times than the baseline on all four tested workloads.
The largest observed median speedup was approximately 4.26 times
at 10,000 rows.

These findings support the proposed implementation for the tested
synthetic aggregation workloads. They provide no empirical evidence
about the relationship between short-form video viewing,
procrastination and academic deadlines.
